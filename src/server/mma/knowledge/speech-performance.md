Speech Performance — How a Character Talks Like a Person

Use this module when someone speaks on camera and the video engine — Seedance or Kling — generates the voice. The user's own words and wishes always win: this module shapes HOW a line is performed, and when a script holds more words than its seconds, how it is fitted (below).

Why it exists

A generated voice sounds robotic when every second is filled with words, the pace never changes, nobody breathes and nobody reacts. Seedance and Kling speak the words a shot holds in the seconds the shot has — neither has a speed setting: delivery words colour a voice, words per second set its pace. "Unhurried" cannot slow a row that holds too many words. Breaths, pauses and reactions are written into the timeline as action and time.

Selection rule

Think before the first line: who is this person right now, what is their body doing (still, walking, running, lifting, laughing), what do they feel, how do they naturally talk, and which words in each sentence matter most. Breath, tempo and sentence shape follow from that — for any situation, including ones no example here covers; the examples show the reasoning, they are never a list to pick from. Pace, voice sentence, delivery slot, breaths and pauses on every spoken video; long natural talk when one person talks at length; reply timing and the listener for two or more speakers; energy by cuts for creator formats. When a PACE LAW names a word budget, that number is the ceiling, never the target: the register and the wordless beats below decide how much of it a calm or elegant scene uses — most of it, not a fraction, so the user's own lines survive. A CopyCat recreation keeps its source's exact lines, timing and cuts: use only the voice sentence, the delivery slot and the listener there. An off-screen narrator is the Narrator's line; nobody on screen mouths it. A speaker whose talk runs on over an insert stays their own voice (the talk-across-cuts formula).

The user's lines are theirs: speak them exactly as written, each by its own speaker — never reworded, reordered, merged, given to someone else or added to. When the script holds more words than the seconds carry at a natural pace, keep its lines exact, in order, up to the last whole line the piece holds, and stop there — the rest is for the user's own extend (a SCRIPT LAW, when present, lists the lines). Retype only when the brief asks for a rewrite (rewrite it, shorten it, adapt it, make it fit) — then every beat in order, who says what, the hook, the turn and the ending, names kept, every line a whole sentence in that person's voice. "Natural" or "long" describes how it is said, never a reason to cut what the user wrote. Never speed speech up.

Prompt formulas

Speech Performance | Pace register (one per speaker) | Prompt formula: [Speaker] speaks at [the pace this person and this moment have] — for a sense of scale, an elegant host about 2.0 words a second, calm 2.2, conversational 2.5, a quick creator 2.8, an excited fast talker the brief asks for about 3, pauses included. The physics are fixed: each engine speaks the words a row holds in the seconds it has, and past about 3.3 words a second the voice garbles; everything inside that is the person's choice. An [N]-second row holds at most [rate × N] words, breaths included; when a PACE LAW is present, its numbers replace these. A 30-second two-hander with reactions holds about 60–70 words; one person talking straight through may use the whole budget.

Speech Performance | Voice sentence (once per speaker) | Prompt formula: Before a speaker's first line: [Speaker] speaks [language and accent] in a [age, pitch, texture] voice, at an [elegant / calm / conversational / quick] pace — the pace in words, never a number (a number reads as a metronome). A saved performance signature colours this voice and its habits, never its pace: when the brief asks for a pace, the brief wins. A voice reference clip carries its own speaking rate, so a calm clip gives a calm speaker.

Speech Performance | Delivery slot (every line) | Prompt formula: Seedance — [Speaker]'s line ([2–4 delivery words]): {[exact words]}; the delivery goes once, BEFORE the words, never after the line and never on one word, which Seedance can print as a subtitle. Kling — [Speaker] says ([delivery], [pace]): "[words]", the delivery and pace in the brackets before the line; only a creator-video Kling clip, whose house form opens on its line, puts it right after: "[words]" [delivery, pace, accent]. Pace is plain words chosen for the person (natural pace, unhurried, speaking slowly, quick); "fast" only when the brief asks for a fast talker. Use neutral, observable words (amused, unhurried, warm, teasing, low and certain); strong ones (fanatical, extremely shocked) make faces overreact.

Speech Performance | Breath that fits the body and the moment (always) | Prompt formula: Breathe where THIS person, doing THIS, would need air — never on a timer, never the same gap twice. Ask what their body is doing and how they feel, then place and size each breath from that. For example: a calm talker takes small, quick, mostly invisible breaths at sentence ends, and a slower visible one only before a turn or a confession; a fast or excited talker runs two or three sentences on one breath, then takes a bigger catch-up breath; someone running, climbing or laughing hard breathes inside the phrase, a few words between audible breaths; a nervous person breathes shallow and swallows before the hard line. These show the reasoning, not a menu. The breath is action or time between the words ("a quick breath, then:", "he gulps air between the words"); the mouth is still while no word is spoken — a breath, never mouthing. When the body is out of breath, a longer line breaks where the air runs out — two pieces of the same line with the breath between ({[first words]} he gulps air, then {[the rest]}). When the brief asks for a breath between sentences, give it one. A new speaker opens on an inhale.

Speech Performance | Pauses are time, not punctuation (always) | Prompt formula: Write a pause as action or seconds, never as an ellipsis, a dash or a half-word inside the braces (Mina's renders turned those into a voice cutting off). A short pause is an action before the line — a glance away, a hand that stops. A held beat before a reveal or a punchline is at least one whole second with no line: room tone or one <[SFX]>, every mouth naturally closed, someone reacting — or, when one person is talking, simply a look on a breath; a monologue needs few of these.

Speech Performance | Reply timing (two or more speakers) | Prompt formula: An agreeing reply lands on the last word; a no, a correction or a thinking reply comes after a small action — "[B] tilts her head, then:". Never the same gap twice running. On Kling, write the switch out ("then [B] answers (...): ...") — without it Kling can run both lines into one voice; a back-and-forth needs about 10 seconds.

Speech Performance | Tone moves (every line) | Prompt formula: One stressed word per sentence. A question lifts, a reveal falls quieter, teasing goes lighter, a refusal starts lower. Change the delivery words as the scene turns; when one speech turns, split it where a real speaker would stop, with the new delivery at the turn; inside one person's talk the action between is optional and the talk goes on in the same voice.

Speech Performance | Spoken sentences, never keywords (always) | Prompt formula: People talk in whole sentences in their own words, with the small words that make speech human ("it's got", "kind of", "I mean", a name, a little repetition). A line is never keywords or telegraph fragments ("Softer than 33. Musky. Summer Pink?" is a list, not a person). When a script must shrink, choose what to drop as that person would — a side thought, a repeat, a sentence that adds nothing — and keep what stays whole and sounds like them; never shred every sentence.

Speech Performance | Seconds tell the truth (every spoken row) | Prompt formula: The pace numbers already include the breaths and small pauses between phrases: a row's seconds are its words at that person's pace, and the breath lives inside them, not on top. A glance, a shrug or a held beat that stops the talk for more than a breath gets its own seconds added, never squeezed out of the words; anything done while talking (pouring, holding, applying, walking, a glance mid-sentence) runs under the words in the same row and adds no seconds. Write breaths and actions in the order they happen, never pinned to tenths of a second — the engine cannot hit them, and a stopwatch reads as a timer.

Speech Performance | Tempo moves inside a speech (every speech longer than a sentence) | Prompt formula: Nobody talks at one speed. Decide, sentence by sentence, what this person rushes through and what they lean on — often quicker through what the listener already knows, an aside, a list or excitement, slower on the point, a name, a number, a reveal or a feeling, with a tiny pause before the word that matters. Show it in the delivery slot ("quick and tumbling, then slowing on the name") or split the speech where the tempo turns, each part with its own slot. Even a fast talker slows a touch on the point, a name or a price. The tempo lives in the seconds, not only in the adjectives: a quick stretch gets fewer seconds per word, the slow line more; the row's average stays inside the person's pace, and a quick phrase is paid for by the slower words around it, never by squeezing the whole row.

Speech Performance | Long natural talk (one person talking at length) | Prompt formula: A monologue, a story told to camera or a speech owes no reaction beats and no wordless rows, and may use the whole budget. Whether it carries as one take or cuts on its breaths is read from the brief: a quick creator edit cuts where the breaths would be, a calm, natural or intimate talk carries as one take; when the brief or the story cuts to what the person is doing or holding, the picture cuts to those inserts while the talk runs on as one voice (the talk-across-cuts formula). Split it into its thought groups — about a sentence each, since a long unbroken line loses lip sync, a long sentence splitting where the speaker would breathe — as consecutive lines of the same speaker, with the breath that fits or a small natural action between them where that person would pause (a glance away, a half-smile, a hand that lands), never the same one twice. Not every line opens on a pause: often the next sentence follows right on a quick breath, and a glance or a gesture comes only where that person would really stop. A piece that continues in the next one ends mid-scene, on a natural beat, never on a finale pose. Delivery words on the first line and again only where the tone or tempo turns, so it reads as one person talking, not separate takes. A one-take talk stays one take: the rows are successive moments of the same shot, and on Kling it is written as one shot, with no Shot 1 / Shot 2. On Kling the same voice goes on — [Speaker] takes a quick breath and goes on ([delivery]): "[words]" — never a new speaker.

Speech Performance | The listener (two or more speakers) | Prompt formula: While [A] speaks, [B] keeps [his/her] mouth naturally closed and responds only through eyes and expression, just after the line that caused it — a slow nod, a growing smile. The frame shows the speaker while a line is spoken; cut to the listener only after its last word. When the brief or the story wants the reaction during the line, cut to the listener with their mouth closed while the speaker's voice runs on off-screen.

Speech Performance | Talk across cuts (an insert, a cutaway, a J-cut or an L-cut) | Prompt formula: The voice is one continuous performance; the picture may leave the speaker for what they are doing or holding. Over an insert of hands, a product, a cup or the task, the line is [Speaker]'s voice, off-screen, goes on: {[words]}, and the shot shows no mouth — hands, the object, or a face that stays still and silent. A J-cut starts the next line under the end of the shot before; an L-cut lets a line run on over the start of the next. Split a line across the cut at a phrase boundary and keep its one delivery: the second half goes on without a new label, so the engine never restarts the sentence. Seedance: the line sits in the row or rows where it is heard. Kling: name the voice in full in every shot it is heard, since nothing carries across shots. Never put “voice-over” or “off-screen voice” in the negatives when the speaker's own voice runs off-screen; ban the visual risk instead (a mouth on the insert, hands that mouth nothing).

Speech Performance | Reaction sounds (sparingly) | Prompt formula: A laugh or a sigh is action outside the braces, at a phrase end — "[Speaker] laughs softly after the line." At most one real laugh in 30 seconds. A "haha" the user typed stays inside, verbatim; never add one.

Speech Performance | Energy by cuts, not speed (creator formats the brief wants cut — never a talk the brief wants calm, natural or in one take) | Prompt formula: One short line per 2–4-second row, at most 2.8 words a second. Cut where the breath would be and open the next row on its first word, or let a line run on over the cut (an L-cut) or start under the end of the shot before (a J-cut); sub-second cuts carry no speech. Keep a wordless beat about every 10 seconds. When a rule fixes one row per beat, keep the beat's lines in that row as separate lines with an action between them.

Speech Performance | Conditional negative prompt | Prompt formula: Avoid [fast speech / robotic cadence / monotone delivery / identical gaps between lines / multiple people speaking simultaneously / a listener mouthing another's line / a frozen silent frame]. Remove any that do not apply. Never put a word the script speaks into the negatives — with native audio it steers the voice away from it; name the visual instead (a second person on screen, makeup on her face).

Compact output structure

Spoken row (Seedance): [time] | [shot] | [action, with any breath or glance] | [Speaker]'s line ([delivery]): {[exact words]} | <[SFX]> | Holds: [...]
Spoken beat (Kling): [action, with any breath or glance], then [Speaker], [what they keep doing while they talk], says ([delivery], [pace]): "[words]" — [listener] listens, mouth closed.
Voice over an insert: [time] | [insert of hands, product or object — no face] | [what the hands do] | [Speaker]'s voice, off-screen, goes on: {[words]} | Holds: no mouth in frame.
Wordless row: [time] | [shot] | [reaction], every mouth naturally closed | <[room tone or one SFX]> | Holds: [...]
Long talk (one speaker, Seedance): [time] | [shot] | [breath or small action] | [Speaker]'s line ([delivery]): {[a full sentence or two]} [a quick breath or a glance], then {[the next thought]} | Holds: [...]
Audio direction: each speaker's pace; breaths audible between thoughts; room tone under every pause.

Core default bans

Never write "no dead air", "no pauses", "brisk pickups" or "talks continuously", in the body or the negatives; when the brief itself asks for continuous talk, give it with the words and their seconds, never with those phrases. "Never silent" means no frozen seconds; a breath or a reaction is not silence. A 30-second spoken scene (speech between actions or between people) keeps at least two wordless beats whenever the script leaves room; one person talking at length follows the long-talk formula instead.

Never speed a line up to fit a shot: give it the seconds, or let the piece stop at the last whole line, as above.

Never put delivery, laughs or breaths inside the braces or quotes.
