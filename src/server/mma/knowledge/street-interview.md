# Street Interview Mode — Bulletproof Prompting Library

Use this mode for spontaneous public interviews, vox pops, man-on-the-street questions, couple reactions, event-floor interviews and creator-led street content. A believable street interview is a negotiated space between interviewer, participant, camera, microphone and an environment that does not stop for them.

Follow `seedance-prompting.md` first for blueprint structure, tags, dialogue timing, negatives and reference limits.

## 1. Pick the street-interview register

- **Fast social vox pop:** vertical, compact question/reaction, handheld or phone rig, quick natural energy.
- **Documentary vox pop:** longer answer, less performance, more environment and thinking time.
- **Couple/friend test:** two respondents interrupt, look at each other and share the microphone.
- **Press-line/event interview:** crowded controlled lane, branded background only when referenced, stronger lighting and clearer audio.
- **Nightlife street interview:** louder crowd, mixed light, closer mic, less intelligible background.
- **Public-experiment/blind test:** interviewer manages product/sample and microphone; hand continuity becomes critical.
- **Walk-and-talk:** interviewer and participant move while camera maintains distance; footstep, traffic and framing causality matter.

## 2. Street interviews need five simultaneous systems

1. **Conversation:** question, listening, answer, overlap and reaction.
2. **Microphone choreography:** who holds it, where it points, how quickly it moves.
3. **Camera choreography:** operator distance, framing correction, foreground interruption.
4. **Public environment:** near, middle and far background activity.
5. **Sound perspective:** voices change with distance, direction, occlusion, traffic and wind.

Write all five. Describing only dialogue creates actors on a silent virtual set.

## 3. Build the environment in depth layers

### Near layer: 0–3 metres

- interviewer and respondent;
- microphone and hand;
- passerby crossing close to lens;
- bicycle wheel, shopping bag, coat edge;
- curb, bollard, café chair or pavement texture;
- footsteps with strong transients.

### Mid layer: 3–15 metres

- pedestrians moving on independent paths;
- parked/idling cars;
- shop windows without invented text;
- bus stop, crossing signal, outdoor tables;
- people occasionally glancing, not everyone staring.

### Far layer: 15 metres and beyond

- traffic bed;
- building line;
- distant horns/sirens only when plausible;
- skyline or trees;
- crowd wash and reverberation.

The background should have low narrative importance but real independent motion. Do not choreograph every pedestrian to react to the interview.

## 4. Camera operator physics

Define:

- device: phone, mirrorless camera, ENG camera;
- operator position and height;
- lens/focal length;
- one- or two-handed grip;
- distance from subjects;
- whether operator walks, pivots or stays planted;
- stabilization type;
- who the framing prioritizes;
- how the operator reacts when the mic/subject moves.

Typical social setup:

> Rear main phone camera, vertical, operator 1.3–1.8 m away, chest-height two-handed grip, moderate wide lens, restrained hand tremor, tiny delayed reframes when the active speaker changes.

Typical documentary setup:

> Shoulder-supported camera, 35–50mm equivalent, operator slightly off interviewer’s eyeline, stable medium two-shot with occasional controlled reframe.

Do not ask for a wide two-shot, tight face close-up and visible full microphone arm simultaneously unless the camera changes shots.

## 5. Microphone choreography

A handheld interview microphone is a visible pointer for attention. Track it exactly.

- identify which hand holds it;
- specify grip position;
- begin below the interviewer’s mouth;
- interviewer brings it toward their own mouth for the question;
- after the final question word, arm travels toward the respondent;
- microphone stops roughly 10–25 cm below or beside the respondent’s mouth, not covering the face;
- it pivots toward the next speaker before they start or during natural overlap;
- interviewer’s arm and shoulder absorb the motion;
- the cable/wireless body remains physically consistent;
- no duplicate microphones unless the setup explicitly uses them.

For couples, decide whether:

- one person answers while the other reacts;
- both lean toward the microphone;
- interviewer alternates the mic;
- one interrupts and becomes louder only after entering the pickup zone.

## 6. Voice distance and microphone physics

Microphone distance is audible.

- interviewer speaking 10–15 cm from mic: present, clear, slightly fuller;
- respondent at 15–25 cm: clear but a little more environment;
- respondent speaking before mic arrives: first word quieter/more distant, then clearer;
- partner leaning close to interrupt: suddenly more present and possibly slightly louder;
- passerby near microphone: brief foreground voice can cut through;
- distant people: indistinct, never equally clear to main speakers.

An omnidirectional interview mic can be forgiving and natural, but the nearest voice still dominates. Directional microphones reduce side ambience but require more accurate pointing. A visible foam/windshield should reduce wind—not remove all street noise.

Include:

- wind direction and strength;
- microphone wind protection;
- handling noise from grip changes;
- clothing rub only when mic or cable touches clothing;
- automatic gain or limiter response to sudden laughter;
- environmental reflections from walls or covered arcades.

## 7. Dialogue should behave like public conversation

Believable street answers contain:

- a short processing pause;
- eye movement toward interviewer or friend;
- uncertainty before a guess;
- one interruption or overlap at most in a 15-second clip;
- reduced words, contractions and ordinary syntax;
- laughter that changes posture and breathing;
- a response shaped by embarrassment, noise or social awareness.

Do not force filler words everywhere. One “um,” restart or half-laugh can be enough.

### Listening behavior

The silent person must still act:

- mouth closed;
- eyes on speaker, mic or friend;
- small nod or brow reaction;
- posture adjustment;
- no lip-sync to the other person’s line.

### Public self-consciousness

Participants often glance at:

- the microphone;
- camera lens;
- interviewer’s eyes;
- passing traffic;
- their companion before committing to an answer.

This gaze pattern is more realistic than permanent direct-to-camera delivery.

## 8. Handshake and approach logic

When the interview begins with a greeting:

- interviewer approaches from a readable direction;
- respondent notices, slows or turns;
- greeting precedes the question;
- handshake uses the free hand, not the microphone hand unless the grip visibly changes;
- hand contact compresses, elbows flex, then release;
- camera operator adjusts distance after subjects stop;
- first words may be slightly off-mic during the greeting.

Avoid instant consent: participants should show a brief recognition/acceptance beat unless the clip begins after permission was already obtained.

## 9. Ambience choreography

Street ambience should change when the scene changes.

- car passes: rises from one stereo side, peaks, recedes;
- bus temporarily masks distant speech and changes exposure if it blocks light;
- pedestrian crosses close: footsteps and clothing become briefly prominent;
- traffic light changes: background movement responds after a realistic delay;
- door opens: short interior music/chatter leak, then closes;
- wind gust: hair, coat fabric, tree leaves and microphone all react together;
- emergency siren: Doppler-like approach/recede only when visible or spatially plausible.

Do not loop identical traffic or crowd sounds. Avoid decorative honks every two seconds.

## 10. Shot grammar

### One-take social interview

- medium two-shot or three-shot;
- active-speaker reframes of only a few degrees;
- no impossible coverage changes;
- microphone and hands remain visible enough for continuity.

### Edited interview

Suggested coverage:

1. contextual wide establishing street and group;
2. medium interviewer question;
3. respondent close or over-shoulder answer;
4. couple reaction/two-shot;
5. product/result insert only during silence or voice-over.

Keep eyelines and screen direction consistent. Do not cross the axis randomly.

## 11. Product/blind-test interviews

For fragrance, food, cosmetics or objects:

- track the product state and who holds it;
- product should not teleport between interviewer and respondent;
- spraying, smelling, tasting or touching needs a full physical sequence;
- avoid placing product at the mouth during dialogue;
- let the respondent react after sensory exposure, not before;
- preserve label orientation and brand-bleed rules;
- one participant can react differently; perfect agreement feels scripted.

## 12. AI tells and fixes

### Silent frozen street
Cause: background described as “busy” without independent actions.
Fix: assign 3–5 subtle timed events across depth layers.

### Everyone is microphone-clear
Cause: one global dialogue instruction.
Fix: write distance, direction and mic arrival for every voice.

### Microphone teleports
Cause: question/answer timing without arm travel.
Fix: include the 0.2–0.5s transfer and shoulder/elbow motion.

### Interviewer and respondent both lip-sync
Cause: model cannot identify active speaker.
Fix: name speaker in each timecode and explicitly freeze non-speaker mouth.

### Constant crowd staring
Cause: “people watch the interview.”
Fix: most passers ignore it; only one brief glance.

### Fake traffic
Cause: generic moving cars with no sound/light consequence.
Fix: connect pass-by position, sound level, reflection and temporary occlusion.

### Influencer performance
Cause: perfect eye contact, fast CTA, huge reactions.
Fix: interviewer can be energetic; respondents should process, glance, hesitate and finish naturally.

### Clean studio audio outdoors
Cause: no microphone or environment logic.
Fix: use realistic close-mic advantage while retaining traffic, wind and reflection.

### Bad group anatomy
Cause: three people, product and mic all moving simultaneously.
Fix: stage one action leader at a time and keep hands visible.

## 13. Prompt assembly template

1. Blueprint and interview register.
2. Cast and reference tags.
3. Location in near/mid/far layers.
4. Camera operator and lens.
5. Microphone type, hand and pickup behavior.
6. Dialogue, speaker timing and non-speaker mouth rules.
7. Timed background events.
8. Product/action continuity when relevant.
9. Audio hierarchy and wind protection.
10. Lighting/grade and imperfection budget.
11. Negative prompt.

## 14. Research principles incorporated

Professional location-interview guidance consistently prioritizes close microphone placement, wind protection, monitoring, handling-noise control and choosing a position that balances useful ambience against noise. Handheld omnidirectional reporter microphones are designed to be forgiving during rapid speaker changes, but distance and pointing still matter. This module converts those real constraints into visible and audible prompt instructions.

## 15. Full Seedance example — 15-second couple fragrance blind test

Vertical 9:16, 15 seconds, 4K captured as a lightly compressed social video, one continuous handheld street-interview take. Early-evening city-centre pavement after light rain. A female creator asks a couple to smell @Image 3 and guess its price. The mood is spontaneous, slightly awkward and funny, not a polished advertisement. The street continues independently around them. Exact speaker-specific lip-sync, physically continuous microphone transfer, realistic microphone distance and layered public ambience are critical. No captions, subtitles, emojis, graphic price text, watermark or background music.

Interviewer — @Image 1 is a British woman in her late twenties, shoulder-length warm brown hair with wind-moved flyaways, natural skin texture, black leather jacket over a plain grey top, straight dark jeans, small silver hoops. She holds one black handheld reporter microphone with a plain foam windscreen in her right hand and @Image 3 in her left. Maintain identical identity, wardrobe, jewellery and hand roles.

Respondents — @Image 2 contains the same couple throughout. Woman: late twenties, curly dark-blonde hair, cream wool coat, burgundy scarf, natural makeup. Man: early thirties, short black hair, navy waterproof jacket, light stubble. They stand close but not posed. She is slightly nearer the microphone; he stands half a step camera-right. Preserve their identities, clothes, height relationship and positions unless movement is described.

Product — @Image 3 is the referenced fragrance bottle. Preserve exact silhouette, front label, cap, colour and scale. It is the only branded object. The bottle remains in @Image 1’s left hand, front mostly toward camera but not deliberately hero-centred. No rotation to an invented back, no duplicate bottle and no brand bleed onto clothing, bags, microphone or shops.

Location and depth. Near layer: wet textured pavement, curb, one plain metal bollard camera-left, the three subjects and microphone. Mid layer: two pedestrians walking independently behind from left to right, one parked dark car, one cyclist passing at 9–11s. Far layer: moving traffic, warm shop-window glow without readable names, bus lights and damp building reflections. Most passers ignore the interview. Only one pedestrian gives a brief glance while continuing to walk.

Camera. A fourth person films on a rear-main-camera phone from about 1.6 metres away at chest height, vertical two-handed grip, moderate wide perspective around 26mm equivalent. Medium three-shot from upper thighs to head. Restrained hand tremor and breath drift. The operator prioritizes the active speaker with delayed 3–5-degree reframes, never zooms and never performs a cinematic orbit. Damp pavement reflections remain geometrically stable.

Microphone and sound. One handheld omnidirectional reporter mic in @Image 1’s right hand, foam windscreen fitted. The interviewer speaks with the mic 12–15 cm below her own mouth, then physically extends it toward the answering person, stopping 15–22 cm below their mouth. Voices become clearer only after the mic arrives. Light wind from camera-left moves hair and coat edges and creates only a low protected rustle, not heavy buffeting. The nearest speaker dominates; partner, pedestrians and traffic sit at realistic distances. Small handling thump occurs only when @Image 1 changes grip.

0.00–3.20s — The recording starts after permission has already been given. @Image 1 faces the couple, not the camera, with the mic beneath her own mouth and @Image 3 held casually at waist level. She asks, “Okay—smell this and tell me what you think it costs.” Natural lively British delivery, but not shouting. On “smell this” she raises @Image 3 to chest level. The couple listen with mouths closed; the woman looks at the bottle, the man glances briefly at his partner. At the final word “costs,” @Image 1 begins lowering the mic and turning her right shoulder toward them. SFX: her voice close and present, shoes on damp pavement, low traffic wash, distant crossing beep.

3.20–6.10s — @Image 1 finishes extending the mic toward the woman respondent while holding @Image 3 under the woman’s nose with her left hand. The respondent leans forward slightly, inhales once, then leans back before speaking. The mic stops about 18 cm below her mouth. She pauses 0.4 seconds, eyes lifting as she thinks, and says, “Honestly? Like… a hundred and twenty?” Her first “Honestly?” begins just as the mic settles and is slightly quieter than the rest. The interviewer and man keep mouths closed. A pedestrian passes in the mid-background without reacting.

6.10–9.50s — Before the interviewer can reply, the man leans 15 cm toward the existing microphone and interrupts with a half-laugh: “No way—eighty, max.” Because he enters the pickup zone, “No way” becomes suddenly clearer and slightly louder; a gentle limiter catches the laugh without distortion. The interviewer pivots the mic only a few degrees toward him rather than teleporting it. The woman turns to him with an amused asymmetric smile and says nothing. @Image 1 lowers the bottle back to mid-chest, maintaining its identity.

9.50–12.40s — @Image 1 brings the microphone back toward herself in a visible 0.35-second arc. A cyclist passes behind from camera-right to left; tyre hiss on wet road rises and recedes while the camera operator makes a small delayed correction to keep the cyclist from obscuring the respondents. @Image 1 says, “It’s twenty-five.” Her delivery is simple and slightly amused, not a sales reveal. The couple’s mouths stay closed until the line is complete; both eyebrows lift, but their reactions are different in timing.

12.40–15.00s — The woman respondent leans toward the mic first and says, “Wait, actually?” while the man laughs behind her and adds a quieter overlapping “That’s mad.” @Image 1 holds the microphone between them rather than choosing perfectly. The woman’s closer voice is dominant; the man’s line is slightly off-axis and lower. @Image 1 gives a small natural nod but no CTA, no product presentation and no look into the camera. At 14.65s a nearby car tyre crosses a shallow puddle, creating a brief wet pass-by sound as the clip ends mid-laughter.

Lighting and image. Overcast blue-hour sky with warm shop bounce. Phone-style HDR keeps faces readable but allows small bright window areas to clip. Wet pavement reflects stable warm shapes. Natural skin texture, light nose/forehead shine, damp flyaways and coat fibres. No cinematic golden-hour grade, no huge bokeh, no artificial rim light.

Audio hierarchy. Main speaker through the handheld mic is always clearest. Off-axis partner voice remains audible but less present. Traffic, cyclist and pedestrians form a changing environmental bed, never equal to speech. No invisible lavaliers, studio bass, noise-free vacuum, applause, music or enhanced comedy SFX.

Imperfection budget. Preserve a delayed camera reframe, one small mic handling thump, unequal reaction timing, slight question pause, wind-moved flyaways, damp shoe sounds, brief speech overlap and one passing foreground acoustic event. Keep dialogue intelligible despite these imperfections.
